If the Ring Road is Iceland's front door, the highlands are the hidden room that most visitors never find. A vast, roadless interior of volcanic peaks, steaming geothermal vents, ancient lava fields, and otherworldly colour — the Icelandic highlands are unlike anything else on Earth.

But they demand respect. The highlands are remote, unpredictable, and unforgiving. Here's how to experience them safely.

## What Are the Highlands?

The Icelandic highlands (hálendið) refer to the interior plateau that sits above 400–600 metres elevation. It covers roughly one-third of Iceland's total area but is home to almost no permanent settlements. Access is only possible via F-roads — mountain tracks that are:

- **Only open in summer** (roughly late June to September, dates vary by road)
- **Passable only by 4WD vehicles with high clearance**
- **Often involve unbridged river crossings**
- **Completely unmarked in places**

There are no petrol stations, no shops, no phone signal, and no guardrails. This is the real deal.

## The Main Highland Routes

### Kjölur Route (F35)
The most accessible highland route. Runs through the centre of Iceland from Gullfoss in the south to Blönduós in the north. Passable with most 4WDs, no major river crossings. The hut and campsite at Hveravellir (a geothermal hot spring oasis) is the highlight.

### Sprengisandur Route (F26)
Crosses Iceland's most desolate landscape — the Sprengisandur sand desert. Long, featureless driving punctuated by mountain views. For experienced off-road drivers only.

### Landmannalaugar
One of Iceland's most famous destinations and the starting point of the Laugavegur Trek. Colourful rhyolite mountains, natural hot springs, and exceptional hiking. The campsite at Landmannalaugar is one of the best in Iceland.

**Laugavegur Trek**: 55 km, 4–5 days, from Landmannalaugar to Þórsmörk. One of Europe's great hikes. Book hut accommodation well in advance, or wild camp — but you'll need serious gear.

### Þórsmörk (Thorsmork)
A forested valley surrounded by glaciers, accessible only by specially modified high-clearance 4WD or on foot. Stunning. Campsites available at Langidalur, Húsadalur, and Básar.

## Essential Gear for the Highlands

The highlands demand better gear than the coastline. Temperatures are lower, winds are stronger, and there are no services to rescue you if your tent fails.

**Tent**: You need a proper expedition tent. Hilleberg is the gold standard — the Hilleberg Nallo, Keron, or Soulo are all excellent. At minimum, use a properly pegged 4-season dome tent. Tunnel tents perform better in high winds but require careful pitching.

**Sleeping bag**: Temperatures in the highlands regularly drop to 0°C and below at night, even in July. Use a bag rated to at least −10°C or layer a liner in a −6°C bag.

**Layers**: The golden rule of highland dressing is layers. Merino wool base layer, fleece mid-layer, waterproof hardshell outer. Never rely on a single heavy jacket.

**Navigation**: Do not rely solely on your phone. Download offline maps (Maps.me or Gaia GPS), carry a physical map and compass, and know how to use them. Phone batteries drain fast in the cold and signal is nonexistent.

**Satellite communicator**: We strongly recommend renting a **Garmin inReach Mini 2** for any highland trip. It allows two-way text messaging and SOS activation from anywhere on Earth. We include it in our Group Explorer bundle and rent it separately.

**Water**: Streams and rivers in the highlands are generally safe to drink from, but we recommend a filter for peace of mind. The Katadyn BeFree is lightweight and fast.

**River crossing gear**: If your route involves river crossings (most do), carry a trekking pole and wear gaiters. Unbuckle your pack's hip belt before entering any river so you can shed it if you fall. Never cross alone.

## Rules and Regulations

The Icelandic highlands are protected land. Strict rules apply:

- **Camping is only permitted at designated campsites** in most highland areas. Check maps carefully.
- **No campfires** — ever. The vegetation is incredibly fragile and fire risk is high.
- **Stay on marked paths** — driving or walking off-trail damages the delicate highland ecosystem, which can take decades to recover.
- **Pack everything out** — there are no bins. Leave no trace, absolutely.

## Safety: The Non-Negotiables

1. **Register your trip** at safetravel.is before you go.
2. **Check F-road status** at vegagerdin.is every morning.
3. **File a detailed itinerary** with someone back home — your planned route, campsites, and expected return date.
4. **Weather check**: highland weather can change from sunny to white-out conditions in 30 minutes. The app Vedur (the Icelandic Met Office) is your best source.
5. **Never ford a river you're unsure about** — wait for conditions to improve, or turn back.
6. **Travel with others** if possible. Solo highland camping significantly increases risk.

## When to Go

- **Late June to early September** is the highland season.
- **July** is the most reliable month — F-roads are generally open, days are longest, and temperatures are warmest.
- **August** sees the highlands at their most dramatic as colours shift and the first autumn hues appear.
- **Never attempt highland camping in winter** without mountaineering-level experience and equipment.

## The Reward

All the preparation is worth it. There is nowhere on Earth quite like Iceland's highlands. Standing on the edge of a steaming geothermal field at midnight under a pale yellow sky, or waking up to a view of Hekla volcano with no other humans in sight — these are experiences that stay with you forever.
